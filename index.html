Body

10 Human Body Facts That Sound Fake But Are True

10 facts · 5 min read

Your body is running thousands of processes right now without asking for your attention. These ten facts are well documented in medical and biological research, and a few of them might change how you look at yourself.

Background

The body is organised in levels. Cells form tissues, tissues form organs, and organs work together in systems such as the circulatory, respiratory, digestive and nervous systems. Anatomy studies the structure of these parts, while physiology studies how they work. Most numbers you see in popular body facts are averages, and real values change with age, sex, genetics and lifestyle, which is why many facts here come as ranges. Modern tools such as X-rays, CT scans and MRI let doctors look inside the living body without surgery, and that is how much of this knowledge was built.

1

Babies have more bones than adults

A newborn has roughly 270 to 300 bones and bony pieces, many of them joined by soft cartilage. As a child grows these fuse together, ending at 206 bones in a typical adult. The gaps between the skull plates are also useful, because they let the head squeeze through the birth canal and leave room for the brain to grow quickly.

2

Your brain is a power hog

An adult brain weighs about 1.3 to 1.4 kilograms, around 2% of body weight, yet it uses roughly 20% of the energy your body burns at rest. It runs mostly on glucose and needs a steady supply of oxygen. Its power use is often compared to a dim light bulb, somewhere around 20 watts.

3

You are taller in the morning

During the day, gravity and your own movement squeeze the soft discs between your spinal bones, so you lose a little height. Overnight, lying down lets those discs take up fluid again. Most people are around a centimetre taller soon after waking than they are by evening.

4

Your heart never takes a day off

At rest, a healthy adult heart beats about 60 to 100 times a minute, which adds up to roughly 100,000 beats a day. Over a long life that is billions of beats. The blood it pumps travels through a network of vessels whose total length is often estimated at around 100,000 kilometres, though exact figures vary between sources.

5

Skin is your largest organ

In an adult, skin covers somewhere between 1.5 and 2 square metres and makes up a noticeable share of body weight. It blocks germs, controls temperature and senses touch. The outer layer is constantly shed and replaced, with a full renewal taking roughly a month.

6

Your stomach lining keeps replacing itself

Stomach acid is strong enough to break down food, so the stomach protects itself with a thick layer of mucus. The cells of the lining are also replaced very quickly, within a few days. When that protective system fails, painful sores called ulcers can form.

7

The thigh bone is a heavyweight champion

The femur is the longest bone in the body, about a quarter of a person's height, and one of the strongest. It can handle forces many times your body weight when you run or jump. Doctors and forensic scientists also use its length to estimate a person's height.

8

Fingerprints form before you are born

Fingerprint ridges develop in the womb, roughly between the 10th and 17th week of pregnancy. They are shaped by a mix of genes and the conditions inside the womb, which is why even identical twins have different prints. Once formed, the pattern stays with you for life.

9

Blinking is a hidden cleaning system

Most people blink around 15 to 20 times a minute without noticing. Each blink spreads a thin film of tears over the eye. That film has an oily layer, a watery layer and a mucus layer that together keep the surface smooth, moist and clear.

10

You are mostly made of tiny cells

Scientists estimate that an adult body contains around 30 to 40 trillion human cells, and the majority of them are red blood cells. The body also hosts a similar number of microbes, mostly bacteria in the gut. Many of them help with digestion and protect against harmful germs.

Common myths and misunderstandings

We use only 10% of our brain

Brain imaging shows that nearly all regions of the brain are active over the course of a day, and even during sleep many areas stay busy. There is no hidden 90% waiting to be unlocked.

Cracking your knuckles causes arthritis

The popping sound comes from gas bubbles in the joint fluid. Studies so far have not found a link between knuckle cracking and arthritis.

Frequently asked questions

How many bones does an adult have?

A typical adult has 206 bones. Some people have extra small bones or an extra rib, so the exact count can differ slightly.

What is the largest organ inside the body?

The skin is the largest organ overall. Among the organs inside the body, the liver is the largest.

How many times does the heart beat in a lifetime?

At roughly 100,000 beats a day, a heart that works for 70 to 80 years beats about 2.5 to 3 billion times.

How much of the body is water?

Water makes up around 60% of an adult's body weight, though the share varies with age and body composition.

Try this yourself

Try a small experiment at home. Make a pencil mark on a wall at your height right after waking up, then measure again at night and compare. You can also count your pulse for 30 seconds and double it to get your resting heart rate, which is normally between 60 and 100 beats a minute. If you have any health concerns, talk to a doctor rather than relying on web articles.

The human body is full of systems that quietly protect and repair themselves. Next time you wake up a little taller or blink without thinking, remember how much work is happening behind the scenes.

Good places to verify body facts: the NHS, Mayo Clinic, the US National Institutes of Health and the World Health Organization. Numbers are rounded and some figures vary between sources.

Space

10 Space Facts That Make Earth Feel Small

10 facts · 5 min read

Space is full of numbers that are hard to picture. These ten facts come from NASA, ESA and other space agencies, and each one is explained in plain language so you can see why it is surprising.

Background

The Sun holds about 99.8% of all the mass in our solar system, and eight planets orbit it. Distances in space are so large that scientists use special units: an astronomical unit is the average Earth-Sun distance of about 150 million kilometres, and a light-year, the distance light travels in a year, is about 9.46 trillion kilometres. Space exploration is a global effort led by agencies such as NASA, ESA, ISRO, JAXA and CNSA. In August 2023, India's Chandrayaan-3 landed near the Moon's south polar region, a first for any country. Numbers in astronomy are refined as new missions and telescopes collect better data.

1

A day on Venus is longer than its year

Venus rotates incredibly slowly, taking about 243 Earth days to spin once. It orbits the Sun in about 225 Earth days, so its day really is longer than its year. It also spins backwards compared with most planets, so the Sun would appear to rise in the west.

2

Sunlight is already 8 minutes old

The Sun is about 150 million kilometres away, and light takes a little over 8 minutes to cover that distance. When you look at the Sun, you see it as it was more than 8 minutes ago. Please do not stare at it directly, as it can seriously damage your eyes.

3

The Moon is slowly drifting away

Laser beams fired at mirrors left on the Moon by Apollo missions show that it moves about 3.8 centimetres farther from Earth every year. The same tidal pull slows Earth's spin very slightly, making our days a tiny fraction of a second longer each century.

4

Footprints on the Moon last a very long time

The Moon has almost no air, no wind and no flowing water to wipe away marks. Only tiny meteorite impacts and temperature swings slowly wear surfaces down. Because of this, astronaut footprints are expected to last for an extremely long time, possibly millions of years.

5

Saturn is less dense than water

Saturn is mostly hydrogen and helium, which makes its average density lower than that of water. That does not mean you could float it, since no ocean is large enough. It is a fun way to show how different gas giants are from rocky planets like Earth.

6

Mars has the tallest volcano we know of

Olympus Mons on Mars rises around 22 kilometres above its surroundings and is about 600 kilometres wide. That makes it roughly two and a half times as tall as Mount Everest is above sea level. Mars has weaker gravity and no moving plates, so its volcano could keep growing in one place.

7

A teaspoon of neutron star is unimaginably heavy

A neutron star is what remains after a massive star collapses. It packs more than the mass of the Sun into a ball only about 20 kilometres across. A single teaspoon of its material would weigh billions of tonnes on Earth.

8

Space is not completely silent

Sound needs air or another medium to travel, so you cannot hear an explosion in the vacuum of space. However, spacecraft can record electromagnetic waves in plasma around planets. Scientists convert these signals into audio, which is how you can hear eerie recordings from Jupiter and Saturn.

9

There are more stars than grains of sand

Researchers have estimated that all the beaches and deserts on Earth hold roughly 7.5 quintillion grains of sand. Estimates for stars in the observable universe range from 10 sextillion up to a septillion, which is far higher. These are rough estimates, but the scale is clear.

10

Astronauts grow taller in space

Without gravity pressing down on the spine, the discs between the vertebrae expand. Astronauts can gain a few centimetres in height during a mission, and some reports mention up to about 5 centimetres. They return to normal within a few months after coming home.

Common myths and misunderstandings

Seasons happen because Earth moves closer to the Sun

Seasons are caused by the tilt of Earth's axis, about 23.4 degrees. Earth is actually slightly closer to the Sun in early January, in the middle of the northern winter.

There is no gravity in space

Gravity is still strong where the International Space Station orbits. Astronauts float because the station and everyone in it are in continuous free fall around Earth.

Frequently asked questions

How many planets are in the solar system?

There are eight planets. Pluto was reclassified as a dwarf planet by the International Astronomical Union in 2006.

How far away is the Moon?

The Moon is about 384,400 kilometres from Earth on average.

What is a light-year?

It is a measure of distance, not time. One light-year is the distance light travels in a year, roughly 9.46 trillion kilometres.

How long does it take to reach Mars?

Uncrewed missions usually take somewhere between six and ten months, depending on the launch window and the route.

Try this yourself

On a clear night, look at the Moon through a pair of binoculars. You can see craters and the dark plains called maria quite clearly. Use a free sky map app to find bright planets such as Venus or Jupiter. Never look at the Sun directly, with or without binoculars.

Facts like these remind us how special and small our planet is. Space science keeps changing, so newer missions may update some of these numbers over time.

Good places to verify space facts: NASA, the European Space Agency and ISRO. Numbers are rounded and some figures vary between sources.

India

10 Amazing Facts About India You May Not Know

10 facts · 5 min read

India has contributed ideas, games and inventions that people around the world still use every day. These ten facts come from well-known history, mathematics and public records.

Background

India has a long record of contributions to mathematics, medicine, metallurgy and astronomy. The astronomer and mathematician Aryabhata wrote the Aryabhatiya in 499 CE and discussed the idea that Earth rotates on its axis, and India's first satellite, launched in 1975, was named after him. Ancient texts such as the Charaka Samhita and the Sushruta Samhita record medical knowledge. In the modern era India is known for its space programme, information technology and digital payments such as UPI. Dates for many ancient works are debated by scholars, so it is best to treat very precise dates with caution.

1

Chess began in India

The ancestor of modern chess is called chaturanga and is believed to have been played in India by around the 6th century. It had pieces that represented the parts of an army. The game then travelled to Persia, the Arab world and finally Europe, where the rules slowly changed into the modern game.

2

Zero and the decimal system have Indian roots

Indian scholars developed place-value numbers and treated zero as a true number. The mathematician Brahmagupta described rules for arithmetic with zero in 628 CE. The ten-digit system then spread through the Arab world to Europe, which is why we call our numerals Hindu-Arabic.

3

The word shampoo is from Hindi

English borrowed the word shampoo from the Hindi word champo, which means to press or massage. In the 1700s, British visitors in India encountered head massage with oils and herbs and took both the practice and the word back home. Over time the meaning shifted to hair washing.

4

Ancient India wrote about surgery

The Sushruta Samhita is an ancient Sanskrit medical text. It is often described as listing more than a hundred surgical instruments and hundreds of procedures, including operations on the nose and eye. Exact dates are debated by scholars, but it is clearly one of the oldest surgical texts in the world.

5

Indian wootz steel was prized worldwide

Wootz was a high-quality crucible steel made in southern India. Traders carried it across the Middle East, where it was forged into famous blades. Its patterned surface and sharpness fascinated metalworkers and scientists for centuries.

6

Varanasi is among the oldest living cities

Varanasi on the banks of the Ganga has been inhabited for roughly three thousand years or more. It remains an important centre of pilgrimage, music, learning and silk weaving. Few cities in the world have had such a long, continuous story.

7

Indian Railways is one of the biggest in the world

The network stretches across the country with tens of thousands of kilometres of route, and it carries on the order of tens of millions of passengers every day. It is also one of the largest employers in the world, with more than a million workers.

8

Ludo comes from an Indian game

Ludo is a simplified version of pachisi, a board game played in India for many centuries. Legend says the Mughal emperor Akbar played a life-size version in the courtyards of Fatehpur Sikri. The British later patented a simplified version under the name Ludo.

9

India has an enormous postal network

India Post runs one of the largest postal systems in the world, with over 150,000 post offices. Most of them are in rural areas, where a post office is often the nearest government service. It also offers savings schemes and banking services.

10

Mangalyaan reached Mars on the first try

In 2014, India's Mars Orbiter Mission reached Martian orbit, making ISRO the first space agency to do so on its very first attempt. It was also notable for its low cost, which was widely reported to be well below that of similar missions from other countries.

Common myths and misunderstandings

Ancient India had every modern machine

Social media often claims that ancient Indians had airplanes, nuclear weapons or the internet. There is no archaeological evidence for such claims. Real achievements, such as zero, place-value numbers, wootz steel and early surgery, are impressive enough without exaggeration.

Frequently asked questions

Who invented zero?

No single inventor is known. Indian mathematicians developed a zero symbol and rules for using it over several centuries, and Brahmagupta wrote clear rules for arithmetic with zero in 628 CE.

Is Varanasi the oldest city in the world?

It is among the oldest continuously inhabited cities, but other cities such as Damascus and Jericho also have strong claims, so scholars avoid naming a single winner.

How many languages are officially recognised in India?

The Constitution's Eighth Schedule lists 22 languages, while hundreds more are spoken across the country.

How many time zones does India have?

India uses one standard time for the whole country, Indian Standard Time, which is 5 hours 30 minutes ahead of UTC.

When did India's Mars mission reach Mars?

Mangalyaan entered Mars orbit on 24 September 2014.

Try this yourself

Play a game of chess or ludo with family or friends, then look up how the rules changed from the old Indian versions. Seeing a game you play today traced back through centuries makes history feel much closer.

From games to mathematics to space missions, India's story keeps surprising people. If a fact interests you, try finding the original source and reading more about it.

Good places to verify these facts: ISRO, Indian Railways, India Post, the Archaeological Survey of India and university history departments. Numbers are rounded and some figures vary between sources.

Animals

10 Animal Facts That Prove Nature Is Weird

10 facts · 5 min read

From three hearts to cube-shaped droppings, the animal kingdom keeps surprising scientists. Every fact below comes from published wildlife and biology research.

Background

Biologists divide animals into vertebrates, which have a backbone, and invertebrates, which do not. Vertebrates include mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians and fish, but the vast majority of animal species are invertebrates, especially insects. Animals have adapted to their surroundings through natural selection over millions of years, which explains oddities like the octopus's copper-based blood. Many species are under threat from habitat loss, and the IUCN Red List tracks which are endangered. India is home to many protected species, and its tiger population, counted in the thousands, is one of the most closely watched conservation stories in the world.

1

Octopuses have three hearts

Two hearts pump blood through the gills and one sends it around the rest of the body. Octopus blood is blue-green because it carries oxygen using copper-based haemocyanin instead of iron-based haemoglobin. The main heart even stops while swimming, which is one reason they prefer crawling.

2

Wombats make cube-shaped droppings

Wombats are among the few animals known to produce cube-shaped poop. Researchers who studied their intestines found that parts of the gut stretch differently, which shapes the waste into cubes. Wombats stack the cubes on rocks and logs to mark their territory, and the shape stops them rolling away.

3

Sharks are older than trees

Fossil evidence suggests sharks have swum in the oceans for more than 400 million years. The earliest true trees appeared roughly 385 million years ago. That means sharks survived several mass extinctions and were already here long before forests covered the land.

4

Crows can remember human faces

Researchers in the United States wore masks while trapping crows and later found that the birds scolded people wearing the same mask for years. Young crows that never saw the original event also learned to dislike the mask, so crows seem to pass information to each other.

5

Tardigrades survive almost anything

Tardigrades, also called water bears, are microscopic animals that can dry out almost completely and then revive. In a 2007 European experiment, some survived days exposed to the vacuum of space. They are not invincible, but they are among the toughest animals ever studied.

6

A group of flamingos is a flamboyance

English has some wonderfully fancy collective nouns. A group of flamingos is called a flamboyance, which suits their bright pink feathers. They get that colour from pigments in the algae and tiny shrimp they eat, so a flamingo's diet decides how pink it becomes.

7

Honeybees dance to give directions

When a honeybee finds good flowers, it returns to the hive and performs a waggle dance. The angle of the dance shows the direction relative to the Sun, and the length of the waggle shows distance. The scientist Karl von Frisch won a Nobel Prize in 1973 partly for decoding it.

8

Cats sleep for most of their lives

Many house cats sleep for 12 to 16 hours a day, and kittens and older cats sleep even more. This habit comes from their wild ancestors, who saved energy between bursts of hunting. A lot of that sleep is light dozing, so cats can wake instantly.

9

Dolphins have name-like whistles

Each bottlenose dolphin develops its own signature whistle early in life. Studies suggest other dolphins can recognise and copy these whistles, which may work like names. It is one reason dolphins are considered highly social and intelligent animals.

10

Blue whales are the largest animals ever known

A blue whale can grow to around 30 metres long and weigh well over 100 tonnes, which is bigger than any known dinosaur. Its heart is huge too, and a preserved heart weighed roughly as much as two adult humans. Despite this size, blue whales feed mostly on tiny krill.

Common myths and misunderstandings

Goldfish have a three-second memory

Experiments show goldfish can learn tasks and remember them for weeks or months.

Bats are blind

Bats can see, and many species see quite well. Some also use echolocation to find food in the dark.

Ostriches bury their heads in the sand

They do not. They lower their heads to the ground to eat or tend nests, which can look like hiding from far away.

Frequently asked questions

What is the fastest land animal?

The cheetah, which can reach roughly 100 kilometres an hour in short bursts.

What is the largest land animal?

The African elephant is the largest living land animal.

How many animal species are known?

Scientists have described well over a million animal species, and most of them are insects. Many more are still undiscovered.

Do octopuses really have blue blood?

Yes. Their blood uses haemocyanin, which contains copper and looks blue-green when carrying oxygen.

Try this yourself

Spend fifteen minutes watching the birds or insects near your home and note what each one is doing. Free apps such as Merlin Bird ID can help identify birds by sound or photo. Watching closely is the first step in how real wildlife research begins.

Nature does not follow our expectations, and that is what makes it fascinating. If one of these facts surprised you, try watching a wildlife documentary on the same animal.

Good places to verify animal facts: National Geographic, the Smithsonian, WWF and university zoology departments. Numbers are rounded and some figures vary between sources.

Tech

10 Tech Facts Behind the Gadgets You Use Every Day

10 facts · 5 min read

The phone in your hand has a surprisingly odd family tree. These ten facts are documented moments from computing and internet history, explained without jargon.

Background

Modern computing grew in steps. Early machines used vacuum tubes, such as ENIAC in the 1940s. The transistor was invented at Bell Labs in 1947, the integrated circuit followed in the late 1950s, and the first commercial microprocessor, Intel's 4004, arrived in 1971. Each step made computers smaller, cheaper and faster. Engineers often cite Moore's observation that the number of transistors on a chip doubled roughly every two years, although that pace has been slowing. The internet, a network of networks that began with ARPANET in 1969, and the World Wide Web, a system of linked pages, are related but not the same thing.

1

The first computer mouse was made of wood

Douglas Engelbart and his team built the first mouse prototype in 1964. It had a wooden shell and two wheels instead of a ball or a laser. It was first shown to a wide audience in a famous 1968 demonstration, years before home computers existed.

2

The first text message said Merry Christmas

On 3 December 1992, engineer Neil Papworth sent the first SMS on the Vodafone network in the UK. It said Merry Christmas and was sent to a colleague's phone from a computer. Phones back then could not even type replies easily.

3

The first .com domain was registered in 1985

Symbolics.com is generally recognised as the first registered .com domain name, in March 1985. The web did not exist yet, so there was nothing to browse. Today there are hundreds of millions of registered domain names.

4

Google started with a different name

Google began in 1996 as a Stanford research project called BackRub, because it analysed links pointing to pages. The name Google was chosen the next year and is a playful spelling of googol, the number 1 followed by 100 zeros.

5

QWERTY has a debated origin

The keyboard layout was developed by Christopher Sholes for early typewriters in the 1870s. A popular story says it was made to slow typists down so keys would not jam, but historians debate this. What is clear is that the layout stuck because people were already trained on it.

6

The first website is still online

In 1991 Tim Berners-Lee published the first website at CERN to explain the World Wide Web project. The original page is not the same file, but CERN has restored a copy of it. You can still visit it and see how simple the early web looked.

7

Early hard drives were huge

In 1956 IBM introduced the 350 RAMAC disk storage unit. It was about the size of two refrigerators, weighed over a tonne and stored around five million characters, roughly 4 megabytes. A single modern phone photo can be larger than that.

8

The word robot comes from Czech

The word robot was introduced to the world by Czech writer Karel Capek in his 1920 play R.U.R. It comes from the Czech word robota, which means forced labour. The idea that Karel's brother Josef suggested the word is also widely told.

9

Email is older than the web

Ray Tomlinson sent the first email between two computers on the ARPANET in 1971. He chose the @ symbol to separate the user name from the computer name, a choice we still use. The web came along nearly two decades later.

10

Bluetooth is named after a king

Bluetooth is named after Harald Bluetooth, a Danish king who united parts of Scandinavia in the 10th century. The name was chosen as a code name for wireless connections that unite devices. The Bluetooth logo combines two old Nordic letters, his initials.

Common myths and misunderstandings

The internet and the web are the same thing

The internet is the global network of connected computers. The web is one service that runs on it, using pages and links. Email, video calls and app updates also use the internet.

Incognito mode makes you anonymous

Private browsing mainly stops your browser from saving history on your device. Websites, your internet provider and your employer or school may still see your activity.

Frequently asked questions

Who invented the internet?

No single person did. Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn designed the TCP/IP protocols in the 1970s, and many others built the network. Tim Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web.

What is the difference between a bit and a byte?

A bit is a single zero or one. A byte is a group of eight bits.

When was the first iPhone released?

Apple released the first iPhone in June 2007.

What does URL stand for?

Uniform Resource Locator, which is the address of a page on the web.

Try this yourself

Visit info.cern.ch to see how the early web looked, then compare it with a modern site. Check how much storage your phone has and work out how many of those 1956-era drives it would take to match it. The answer is usually in the thousands of drives or more.

Every device we use today is built on small steps taken by curious people. Understanding that history makes new technology feel a little less mysterious.

Good places to verify tech history: CERN, the Computer History Museum, IBM's archives and Stanford University. Numbers are rounded and some figures vary between sources.

Food

10 Food Facts That Will Change What You Eat

10 facts · 5 min read

Some foods are not what they seem. These ten facts come from food science, botany and history, and several of them will change how you look at your next meal.

Background

Food science combines chemistry, biology and history. Cooking changes food through reactions such as the Maillard reaction, which gives toast and roasted meat their brown colour and flavour. Preservation methods such as salting, drying, pickling and sugaring were developed to stop spoilage long before refrigerators. Classifications also differ between kitchens and botany, which is why a tomato is a fruit to a botanist and a vegetable to a cook. Many foods Indians eat every day arrived from other regions. Chillies, potatoes and tomatoes came from the Americas and reached India after the 16th century, brought by Portuguese traders.

1

Honey keeps for an extremely long time

Honey is very low in water and naturally acidic, which makes it hard for bacteria to grow. Jars of well-preserved honey have been found in ancient Egyptian tombs. Stories about people tasting it are popular, but the real lesson is that sealed honey can last for ages.

2

Bananas are slightly radioactive

Bananas contain potassium, and a tiny fraction of natural potassium is a radioactive isotope called potassium-40. The dose from eating a banana is extremely small, far below anything harmful. Scientists even use the banana as a fun unit to compare tiny radiation doses.

3

Bananas are berries but strawberries are not

Botanists define a berry as a fruit formed from a single flower ovary with seeds inside the flesh. By that definition bananas, grapes and even tomatoes count as berries. Strawberries do not, because the red part grows from the base of the flower and the seeds sit on the outside.

4

Peanuts are not true nuts

Peanuts belong to the legume family, the same group as beans, peas and lentils. The plant flowers above ground, then the stalk bends and pushes the developing pods underground to ripen. That is why peanuts are sometimes called groundnuts.

5

Ketchup was once sold as medicine

In 1834 an American doctor named John Cooke Bennett promoted tomato-based pills as a cure for stomach problems. Tomatoes were still viewed with suspicion by many people at that time. The idea faded, but ketchup later became a popular sauce on its own.

Carrots were not always orange

Early cultivated carrots were often purple or yellow. Orange varieties spread through Europe by the 1600s, and today they are the most common. A popular story links them to the Dutch royal family, but historians say that part is not proven.

Cashew nuts grow outside the fruit

The cashew tree produces a fruit called the cashew apple, and the nut hangs from the bottom of it outside the fruit. The shell contains an irritating oil, so cashews are always processed before sale. That extra work is part of why they cost more than many other nuts.

Birds do not feel chilli heat

Chillies feel hot because of a compound called capsaicin, which triggers heat and pain receptors in the mouth. Birds do not react to it in the same way. This helps the plant, because birds eat the fruit and spread the seeds, while mammals tend to avoid it.

Saffron is expensive for good reason

Saffron comes from the dried stigmas of the crocus flower, and each flower has only three. Hundreds of thousands of hand-picked flowers are needed for one kilogram. Kashmir in India is one of the famous saffron growing regions of the world.

Rice feeds a huge share of the world

Rice is a staple food for roughly half of the world's population, especially across Asia. It grows in many climates, and thousands of varieties exist, from fragrant basmati to sticky short-grain types. India is one of the largest producers and exporters.

Sugar makes children hyperactive

Controlled studies have not found that sugar causes hyperactivity in children. Excitement at parties is a more likely explanation.

Carrots give you night vision

Cutting an onion releases a sulphur compound that turns into a gas irritating the eyes, so your eyes produce tears to wash it away.

Is a tomato a fruit or a vegetable?

Botanically it is a fruit because it develops from a flower and contains seeds. In cooking it is treated as a vegetable.

What is the Maillard reaction?

It is a chemical reaction between amino acids and sugars when food is heated, creating browning and new flavours.

10 Geography Facts That Will Change How You See the Map

10 facts · 5 min read

Maps and globes hide surprising details. These ten facts about oceans, lakes, deserts and countries are drawn from geography and earth science, and a few of them contradict what many people learn at school.

Background

Geography has two broad branches: physical geography, which studies landforms, climate and oceans, and human geography, which studies people, cities and cultures. Every flat map has to distort something, because a sphere cannot be flattened without stretching area, shape or distance. India's geography is remarkably varied, with the Himalayas in the north, the Gangetic plains, the Thar desert, the Deccan plateau and a coastline of about 7,500 kilometres including its islands. The Indian plate is still moving northwards by a few centimetres a year, pushing the Himalayas slowly higher.

1

The deepest place in the ocean is almost 11 kilometres down

The Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench in the western Pacific Ocean is about 10,900 metres deep, give or take a few dozen metres depending on the survey. If Mount Everest were placed inside it, its peak would still be more than a mile underwater.

2

The Pacific Ocean is larger than all the land combined

The Pacific covers roughly 165 million square kilometres. All the continents and islands together add up to about 149 million square kilometres. It is the largest and deepest of Earth's oceans and holds more than half of the free water on the planet.

3

The Dead Sea shore is the lowest land on Earth

The shore of the Dead Sea, between Israel, Jordan and the West Bank, lies more than 400 metres below sea level. The water is so salty that swimmers float easily. The lake has been shrinking in recent decades, which is a growing concern.

4

Russia spans eleven time zones

Russia is the largest country in the world by area, and it stretches across eleven time zones. When people in Kaliningrad are having breakfast, people on the far eastern edge are already well into the next part of the day.

5

Lake Baikal holds a fifth of the world's lake water

Lake Baikal in Siberia is the deepest lake on Earth, at about 1,600 metres, and is estimated to be around 25 million years old. It holds roughly 20% of the planet's unfrozen surface fresh water. It is also home to many species found nowhere else.

6

Antarctica is a desert

A desert is defined by how little precipitation it gets, not by heat. Most of Antarctica receives very little snowfall, so it counts as the largest desert in the world. It also holds around 70% of Earth's fresh water, locked up as ice.

7

Everest is not the tallest mountain from base to peak

Mount Everest is the highest above sea level at about 8,849 metres. But Mauna Kea in Hawaii rises over 10,000 metres from the ocean floor, with most of it hidden underwater. Which mountain is tallest depends on where you start measuring.

Many world maps make countries look the wrong size

The common Mercator map was designed for ship navigation, and it stretches areas near the poles. Greenland looks as large as Africa on such maps, but Africa is actually around 14 times bigger. Many modern atlases now show more balanced projections.

Indonesia is made of thousands of islands

Indonesia is the world's largest island country, with over 17,000 islands. Only a portion of them are inhabited. It lies along the Pacific Ring of Fire, which is why it has many active volcanoes and earthquakes.

Canada has the longest coastline

Mount Everest is the point closest to space

Angel Falls in Venezuela, with a total height of about 979 metres.

Science

10 Science Facts That Sound Impossible But Are Real

10 facts · 5 min read

Science is full of results that seem to break common sense. These ten facts come from physics, chemistry and astronomy, and each one is explained in simple language.

Background

Science works by observing the world, forming a hypothesis, testing it with experiments and sharing results for other scientists to check. A scientific theory is not just a guess. It is a well-tested explanation supported by a large amount of evidence. Physics, chemistry, biology and earth science all follow this approach. India has produced many notable scientists, including C. V. Raman, who discovered the Raman effect in 1928 and won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1930, S. N. Bose, whose work led to Bose-Einstein statistics, and S. Chandrasekhar, who won the Nobel Prize in 1983 for his work on the later stages of stars.

1

Lightning is hotter than the surface of the Sun

A lightning bolt briefly heats the air around it to about 30,000 degrees Celsius. The visible surface of the Sun is around 5,500 degrees Celsius. The bolt lasts only a tiny fraction of a second, and the sudden expansion of hot air is what we hear as thunder.

2

Water can boil and freeze at the same time

At a very specific temperature and pressure called the triple point, water exists as ice, liquid and vapour together. For water this is just above 0 degrees Celsius at a pressure far lower than normal air. Laboratories use it as a precise reference for measuring temperature.

3

Sound travels faster in water than in air

Sound moves at about 343 metres per second in air at room temperature. In sea water it travels at roughly 1,500 metres per second, more than four times faster. That is why whales can communicate over very long distances.

4

Diamonds and pencil lead are both carbon

Diamond and graphite are both made purely of carbon atoms. The difference is how the atoms are arranged. In diamond, each atom is locked into a strong three-dimensional lattice, while in graphite the atoms form flat layers that slide over each other.

5

Glass is not a slow-moving liquid

It is often said that old windows are thicker at the bottom because glass flows over centuries. Scientists say this is a myth. Glass is an amorphous solid, and the uneven thickness in old panes comes from how they were made by hand.

Ad space
6

Light takes thousands of years to leave the Sun's core

Energy made in the core of the Sun is repeatedly absorbed and re-emitted by dense gas before reaching the surface. Estimates for this journey range from tens of thousands to well over 100,000 years. After that, it takes only about 8 minutes to reach Earth.

7

Atoms are mostly empty space

An atom has a tiny nucleus surrounded by a cloud of electrons. The nucleus is around one hundred thousand times smaller than the whole atom. Solid things feel solid because of forces between electrons, not because the space is filled.

8

Much of the gold on Earth may come from colliding neutron stars

Heavy elements like gold are not made in ordinary stars. In 2017, astronomers detected a collision between two neutron stars and found signs of freshly made heavy elements. Many scientists now think such collisions are a major source of gold and platinum.

9

Absolute zero is the lowest possible temperature

Absolute zero is minus 273.15 degrees Celsius, the point where particles have the least possible motion. Scientists have cooled matter to temperatures only a tiny fraction above it, but physics says you can never reach it exactly.

10

All humans are about 99.9% the same genetically

Any two people share about 99.9% of their DNA, and the small remainder explains differences in appearance and some health risks. Humans also share a high proportion of DNA with chimpanzees, commonly quoted at around 98 to 99%, depending on how it is measured.

Common myths and misunderstandings

Lightning never strikes the same place twice

Tall structures are hit repeatedly. The Empire State Building in New York is struck around 20 times in a typical year.

Humans have only five senses

Besides sight, hearing, smell, taste and touch, we also sense balance, temperature, pain and the position of our own limbs.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the sky blue?

Sunlight contains all colours. Air molecules scatter short blue wavelengths more strongly than long red ones, so blue light reaches your eyes from every direction.

What is the speed of light?

In a vacuum it is 299,792,458 metres per second, usually rounded to 300,000 kilometres a second.

Why does ice float on water?

When water freezes, its molecules form an open structure that is less dense than liquid water, so ice floats.

How many elements are there?

Scientists have confirmed 118 chemical elements, and about 90 of them occur naturally on Earth.

Try this yourself

Place a clear glass of water on a sunny windowsill with a sheet of white paper behind it and tilt it until you see a rainbow. The curved glass and water bend and spread sunlight into its colours. It is a simple way to see refraction at work.

Science often feels strange because it describes things far bigger, smaller or faster than daily life. Curiosity is the best tool for making sense of it.

History

10 History Facts That Sound Like Myths

10 facts · 5 min read

History is stranger than fiction, and some well-known stories turn out to be wrong. These ten facts are supported by historians and archaeologists, and a few of them correct popular beliefs.

Background

Historians study the past using primary sources, such as letters, inscriptions and official records, together with archaeology. Scientific tools help too. Radiocarbon dating, for example, measures the decay of carbon-14 to date organic material up to roughly 50,000 years old. Dates are written as BCE, before the common era, and CE, common era. Indian history stretches from the Indus Valley cities of about 2600 BCE, through the Maurya empire from around 321 BCE and the Gupta period from around 320 CE, to the Mughal era beginning in 1526. History is revised as new evidence appears, so a good habit is to check where a popular story comes from.

1

Oxford University is older than the Aztec Empire

Teaching at Oxford is recorded as early as 1096, and it grew rapidly after 1167. The Aztec city of Tenochtitlan was founded around 1325. So people were already studying at Oxford for over two centuries before Tenochtitlan existed.

2

Cleopatra lived closer to us than to the pyramids

The Great Pyramid of Giza was built around 2560 BCE. Cleopatra VII lived from 69 to 30 BCE, about 2,500 years later. The Moon landing in 1969 was about 2,000 years after her, so she lived nearer in time to us than to the pyramid builders.

3

The Great Wall is not visible from space with the naked eye

This popular claim is a myth. The wall is long but narrow, and astronauts have said it is very hard or impossible to pick out unaided from orbit. Many other human structures, such as city lights and highways, are easier to spot.

4

Napoleon was not unusually short

Napoleon is often pictured as tiny, but he was about 5 feet 6 or 7 inches in modern measurements, which was average or slightly above average for his time. The confusion comes from the French inch being longer than the English inch and from British cartoons.

5

Vikings did not wear horned helmets

There is no archaeological evidence that Viking warriors wore horned helmets in battle. The image became popular in the 19th century, partly through costume designs for an opera. Real Viking helmets found so far are simple and rounded.

6

The shortest war in history lasted under an hour

The Anglo-Zanzibar War of 27 August 1896 is commonly described as the shortest recorded war. After British warships bombarded the palace, the fighting ended in about 40 minutes, give or take, depending on the source.

7

The Indus Valley cities were carefully planned

Cities like Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, over 4,000 years old, had grid-like streets, brick houses and covered drains. Many homes had their own bathing spaces. Their urban planning surprised archaeologists when they were discovered in the 1920s.

8

Nalanda was one of the world's earliest universities

Nalanda in Bihar flourished from around the 5th century CE and drew students from across Asia, including China and Korea. It had a very large library and many residential halls. It was destroyed in the 12th century, and its ruins are now a World Heritage Site.

9

Roman concrete may heal itself

Many Roman harbours and buildings are still standing after about two thousand years. A 2023 study suggested that small lumps of lime in the concrete could dissolve, then re-harden to seal cracks. Researchers are now exploring how to copy the idea in modern construction.

10

Printing existed long before Gutenberg

Printing with carved blocks was used in China more than a thousand years ago, and movable type was developed there in the 11th century. Johannes Gutenberg's printing press of the 1440s was a major European breakthrough, but he did not invent printing itself.

Common myths and misunderstandings

People in the Middle Ages thought the Earth was flat

Educated Europeans of the time knew the Earth was round. The idea that everyone believed in a flat Earth became popular much later.

Einstein failed mathematics at school

He was excellent at mathematics and had mastered calculus by his mid-teens. The story likely comes from a misunderstanding of grading scales.

Frequently asked questions

Who built the pyramids?

Archaeological evidence from the workers' town near Giza suggests the pyramids were built by organised, fed and housed Egyptian labourers, not by slaves.

When did the Western Roman Empire fall?

The traditional date is 476 CE. The Eastern Roman Empire, also called the Byzantine Empire, lasted until 1453.

Who was Ashoka?

Ashoka was a Maurya emperor who ruled in the 3rd century BCE. After the Kalinga war around 261 BCE, he embraced Buddhism and spread messages of non-violence on rock and pillar edicts.

When did India become independent?

India became independent from British rule on 15 August 1947.

Try this yourself

Visit a nearby museum or heritage site and read the labels carefully. The Archaeological Survey of India website also lists protected monuments. Ask yourself what evidence supports each claim you read.

History is written by people, so it keeps being corrected as new evidence appears. Questioning popular stories is part of understanding the past.

Many modern sports took their current rules in the 19th century, when clubs and national bodies began writing standard laws. India has strong traditions in cricket, hockey, wrestling, badminton, kabaddi and chess, and in recent years Indian athletes have made rapid progress in javelin, shooting and weightlifting. Sachin Tendulkar scored 100 international centuries

India won the 1983 Cricket World Cup as underdogs

India has won eight Olympic gold medals in hockey

Cricket has been an Olympic sport only once

The marathon distance was fixed by accident of history

Basketball began with peach baskets

Money

10 Money and Business Facts Behind Everyday Life

10 facts · 5 min read

Money feels familiar, but its history is full of surprising turns. These ten facts explain where common financial words, companies and ideas came from, with a focus on India and the world.

Background

Money developed in stages. Early societies used barter, then goods like grain and shells, then metal coins. Coins were minted in Lydia, in present-day Turkey, around the 7th century BCE. Paper money followed in China, and today much of our money exists as digital records. The Reserve Bank of India was founded on 1 April 1935 and acts as the country's central bank. Digital payments have grown quickly in India since the Unified Payments Interface, UPI, was launched by NPCI in 2016. This article is for general education and is not financial advice.

1

Paper money was first used in China

Merchants in Song dynasty China began using paper notes around the 11th century, because carrying heavy strings of coins was inconvenient. Governments later issued official paper currency. Europe adopted banknotes many centuries after that.

2

The word rupee comes from Sanskrit

The word rupee comes from the Sanskrit word rupya, which refers to silver coin. The ruler Sher Shah Suri introduced a silver coin called the rupiya in the 16th century, and the Mughals continued the system. The name has survived for nearly five hundred years.

3

The rupee symbol is only from 2010

The sign for the Indian rupee was officially adopted in 2010. It was designed by D. Udaya Kumar and blends the Devanagari letter Ra with the Roman letter R, topped by two horizontal lines that echo the Indian tricolour.

4

The first listed company was Dutch

The Dutch East India Company, known as the VOC, started trading its shares publicly in Amsterdam in 1602. It is widely regarded as the first company to issue shares to the general public, which helped create the modern stock market.

5

Bombay's stock exchange is the oldest in Asia

The Bombay Stock Exchange traces its roots to 1875, when a small group of brokers met under a banyan tree. It is often described as the oldest stock exchange in Asia, and it is still one of the key places where Indian companies raise money.

Advertisement
Ad space
6

Nokia started as a paper mill

Nokia was founded in 1865 as a paper mill in Finland, and it later expanded into rubber boots and cables before making phones. It shows how companies can change direction entirely over a century and a half.

7

Nintendo began by making playing cards

Nintendo was founded in 1889 in Kyoto to make handmade Japanese playing cards called hanafuda. It later tried taxis, instant rice and toys before becoming a famous video game company.

8

The word bank comes from a bench

The English word bank comes from the Italian word banco, meaning a bench. Early money changers in medieval Italian cities did business sitting at benches in public squares. The word bankrupt also has links to a broken bench.

9

Diners Club created the first modern credit card

The Diners Club card was introduced in the United States in 1950 and is often called the first general-purpose charge card. Legend says it began after a businessman forgot his wallet at a restaurant, although the story is told in different ways.

10

Zimbabwe once printed a 100 trillion dollar note

During severe inflation in 2008 and 2009, Zimbabwe issued a banknote worth 100 trillion Zimbabwean dollars. At times prices doubled in a single day. In 2009 the country stopped using its own currency for a time, and the note is now a collector's item.

Common myths and misunderstandings

Banks keep all your deposits in a vault

Banks keep a portion as reserves and lend out much of the rest. That is how they earn interest, and it is why deposit insurance and regulation exist.

Banknotes are made of paper

Many banknotes, including Indian ones, are printed on a cotton-based material that is stronger and lasts longer than ordinary paper.

Frequently asked questions

What is inflation?

Inflation is a general rise in prices over time, which means each rupee buys a little less than before.

What is compound interest?

It is interest earned on both your original amount and on the interest already added, which makes savings grow faster over time.

What is the Rule of 72?

Divide 72 by the yearly interest rate to estimate how many years it takes money to double. At 8% it is roughly 9 years.

What is UPI?

The Unified Payments Interface is a system run by NPCI that lets people send money between bank accounts instantly using a mobile phone.

Who issues Indian currency?

The Reserve Bank of India issues banknotes, while coins are minted by government mints.

Try this yourself

Use the Rule of 72 on a few interest rates, such as 6%, 8% and 12%, and see how quickly the doubling time shrinks. Then compare it with how fast prices rise at your usual inflation rate. This is a simple way to see why saving early matters.

Money is a human invention that keeps changing, from silver coins to paper to digital payments. Knowing the story behind it makes everyday finance a little less mysterious.

10 Nature and Weather Facts That Feel Like Magic

10 facts · 5 min read

Plants, rain and storms are full of secrets. These ten facts come from botany and weather science, with several examples from India.

Background

Weather is the day-to-day state of the atmosphere, while climate is the average over many years. In India, the monsoon is the main event. Winds bring moist air from the sea, and the rains usually reach Kerala around the start of June and then spread north, delivering the majority of the country's yearly rainfall. India is also one of the world's megadiverse countries, with habitats from Himalayan forests to coastal mangroves, and the Western Ghats is a UNESCO World Heritage biodiversity hotspot. Scientists keep monitoring changes in rainfall and temperature, so record values may change over time.

1

Some bamboo grows almost a metre in a day

Certain giant bamboo species have been recorded growing around 90 centimetres in 24 hours, making bamboo one of the fastest-growing plants in the world. It is technically a grass and is used for building, paper and food across Asia.

2

A single banyan tree can look like a forest

The Great Banyan in the botanical garden near Kolkata is more than 200 years old and has a huge crown supported by hundreds of aerial roots. It is often listed among the widest trees in the world by canopy area.

3

The smell of rain has a name

The pleasant earthy smell after the first rain is called petrichor, a word created by two Australian scientists in 1964. It comes from oils released by plants and compounds made by soil bacteria that rise into the air when raindrops hit dry ground.

4

Mawsynram is one of the wettest places on Earth

Mawsynram in Meghalaya, India, is often named as the place with the highest average yearly rainfall in the world. The Khasi hills force the moist monsoon winds to rise and release huge amounts of rain every year.

5

Lightning strikes the Earth about a hundred times a second

Scientists estimate that lightning flashes strike the ground and clouds many times every second around the world, and roughly 100 of those reach the ground each second. Most occur over land in the tropics, where warm, moist air builds towering storm clouds.

6

The hottest and coldest recorded temperatures are extreme

The World Meteorological Organization recognises 56.7 degrees Celsius at Death Valley, California, in 1913 as the highest air temperature recorded. The lowest is minus 89.2 degrees Celsius at Vostok Station, Antarctica, in 1983. Some of these records are still debated by experts.

7

The Venus flytrap counts

A Venus flytrap leaf has tiny trigger hairs. To avoid wasting energy on a falling raindrop, it usually snaps shut only if two hairs are touched within about 20 seconds. After that it slowly digests the insect and reopens.

8

Some trees are thousands of years old

A bristlecone pine in California known as Methuselah is estimated to be about 4,800 years old, making it one of the oldest known individual trees. These trees grow slowly in harsh, dry mountain conditions, which helps them live for a very long time.

9

The Sundarbans is the largest mangrove forest

The Sundarbans, shared by India and Bangladesh, is the world's largest mangrove forest. It is famous for its Bengal tigers, which swim between islands. Mangrove roots also protect coastlines from storms and floods.

10

Young sunflowers follow the Sun

Young sunflower buds turn gradually from east to west during the day, then swing back overnight, following the Sun. This behaviour is called heliotropism. Once the flower matures, it usually stops moving and faces east.

Common myths and misunderstandings

The Amazon is the lungs of the Earth

The Amazon produces a lot of oxygen, but its plants and microbes use almost as much. A large share of the oxygen in our air is produced by tiny ocean organisms called phytoplankton.

Frequently asked questions

Why does it rain?

Warm air rises and cools, water vapour condenses into tiny droplets in clouds, and when the droplets grow heavy enough they fall as rain.

What causes thunder?

Lightning heats the air so suddenly that it expands explosively, creating a shock wave that we hear as thunder.

How can I tell how far lightning is?

Count the seconds between the flash and the thunder, then divide by three. The result is roughly the distance in kilometres.

What is a monsoon?

A monsoon is a seasonal change in wind direction that brings a long wet season to regions such as South Asia.

What is the tallest tree?

A coast redwood in California named Hyperion is about 116 metres tall, which is the tallest known living tree.

Try this yourself

Soak a handful of moong beans overnight, place them in a damp cloth or a cup, and watch them sprout over the next few days. Take a photo each day. You will see seed germination, one of nature's most important processes, up close.

Nature runs on tiny chemical signals and powerful forces that we are still learning about. A walk outside after the next rain is a good way to notice a few of them.
