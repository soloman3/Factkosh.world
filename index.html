    How the European Union Actually Works: The Institutions Explained

    Commission, Parliament, Council, Court: who does what in Brussels, and why decisions there can feel so slow.

    Ask ten people in Europe how the European Union makes decisions and you will probably get ten different answers. The system is genuinely unusual: it is not a country, and it is not simply a club of governments either. It sits somewhere in between, and its institutions reflect that. Once you know the five or six bodies that matter, most EU news becomes much easier to follow.

    A union of states, not a state

    The EU grew out of the European Coal and Steel Community of 1951, an effort by six countries to place the raw materials of war under shared control. Through a series of treaties, from Rome in 1957 to Maastricht in 1992 and Lisbon in 2009, member states agreed to share decision making in particular areas such as trade, competition, agriculture and the single market. In other areas, such as defence, taxation and most of foreign policy, governments still hold the main power.

    Today the EU has 27 member states. The United Kingdom left on 31 January 2020, the first and so far only country to do so. Whatever one thinks of that decision, it showed that membership is voluntary and that the treaties contain a formal exit procedure.

    The European Commission: the proposer

    The Commission is often described as the EU's executive, but its most important power is the right of initiative: in most policy areas, only the Commission can formally propose new EU laws. It also monitors whether member states are applying EU rules, negotiates trade agreements on behalf of the Union, and manages the EU budget.

    The Commission has one commissioner from each member state, led by a President. The President is proposed by the European Council and then has to be approved by the European Parliament, which must also approve the whole college of commissioners. The Commission is based in Brussels and employs a large civil service, which is partly why "Brussels" has become shorthand for the EU as a whole.

    The European Parliament: the voice of citizens

    The Parliament is the only EU institution whose members are directly elected by citizens, every five years. Its seats are divided among member states roughly according to population, though smaller countries get proportionally more. Members of the European Parliament sit not in national blocs but in political groups, such as the centre-right, the social democrats, the liberals and the greens.

    Under the ordinary legislative procedure, which covers most EU laws, the Parliament has equal say with the Council. A law cannot pass unless both agree. The Parliament also shares control of the annual budget and supervises the Commission. It has two homes: it holds its main plenary sessions in Strasbourg and much of its committee work takes place in Brussels, an arrangement written into the treaties that critics call wasteful and supporters call a symbol of compromise.

    The Council of the EU: the governments

    Do not confuse this with the next body. The Council of the European Union, sometimes called the Council of Ministers, is where national ministers meet to adopt laws and coordinate policy. Which ministers attend depends on the topic: agriculture ministers for farming, finance ministers for the budget, and so on.

    Votes are usually taken by "qualified majority", which requires at least 55 percent of member states representing at least 65 percent of the EU population. Some sensitive areas, such as foreign policy and taxation, still require unanimity. That requirement is the reason a single government can slow or block a decision, and it is a recurring theme in debates about reform.

    The European Council: the summit

    The European Council is the body made up of the heads of state or government of all member states, together with its own President and the President of the Commission. It meets several times a year and sets the broad political direction of the Union. It does not pass laws. Instead, its conclusions guide what the Commission proposes and what the Council works on.

    Because the two names are so similar, news reports can be confusing. A simple rule: the European Council is the summit of leaders, while the Council of the EU is the regular meeting of ministers.

    The Court of Justice and the others

    The Court of Justice of the European Union, in Luxembourg, interprets EU law and ensures it is applied the same way everywhere. National courts can ask it for rulings, and its decisions have shaped everything from consumer rights to data protection. Alongside it, the European Central Bank in Frankfurt runs monetary policy for the euro area, and the Court of Auditors checks how EU money is spent.

    How a law is made

    A typical EU law follows a recognisable path: - The Commission proposes a regulation or directive after consultation with experts and the public. - The Parliament and the Council each examine and amend the text, often through negotiations between the two known as trilogues. - Both must agree on a final version, which is then formally adopted and published in the Official Journal. - Member states apply it. A regulation applies directly everywhere, while a directive must be turned into national law within a set deadline.

    Why it feels slow

    The process involves 27 governments, a directly elected parliament and a Commission with a formal monopoly on proposals. That makes outcomes slower than in a single country, but it also means that rules adopted have broad legitimacy and apply across a market of roughly 450 million people. Whether the trade-off is worth it is the central argument of European politics, and understanding the machinery is the first step to joining that argument sensibly.

    The Schengen Area Explained: How Europe Removed Its Internal Borders

    You can drive from Lisbon to Helsinki without showing a passport. Here is how that works, who is in, and what the rules really say.

    Few things make the European project feel real to ordinary people as much as crossing a border without stopping. On a road between Germany and Austria, or on a train from Paris to Brussels, you may only notice the change because your phone welcomes you to a new network. This is the Schengen Area, and it is one of the most visible achievements of European integration.

    Where the name comes from

    Schengen is a small village in Luxembourg, on the river Moselle, close to the point where Luxembourg, France and Germany meet. On 14 June 1985, representatives of five countries (Belgium, France, Germany, Luxembourg and the Netherlands) signed an agreement there, on a boat, to gradually abolish checks at their common borders. A more detailed convention followed in 1990, and the arrangement actually took effect in 1995.

    At first it was separate from the European Community. It only became part of EU law with the Treaty of Amsterdam in 1999. That is why membership of Schengen and membership of the EU are not exactly the same thing.

    Advertisement

    Who is in, and who is not

    The Schengen Area now covers more than two dozen European countries. Most are EU members, but four countries outside the EU are also part of it: Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway and Switzerland. At the same time, some EU members are not in the area. Ireland has an opt-out and keeps its own border arrangements with the United Kingdom, and Cyprus is still preparing to join. Others have joined in stages over time, usually first with checks lifted at airports and seaports, then on land borders.

    Tiny states such as Monaco, San Marino and the Vatican City have open borders with their neighbours in practice, though they are not formally members.

    What "no border checks" means

    Inside the area, people, whatever their nationality, can cross internal borders without systematic checks. There are no passport controls at the crossing itself. But this does not mean there are no rules: - You still need ID. Airlines and trains may ask for it, and in many countries you are legally required to be able to prove your identity. - Police checks are still allowed inside the territory, as long as they are not equivalent to border checks. - Temporary border controls can be reintroduced for a limited period if there is a serious threat to public policy or internal security. Several countries have done so, for example during the 2015 migration crisis and during the COVID-19 pandemic.

    The external border

    Removing checks inside the area only works if the outer edge is managed carefully. Each country is responsible for the section of external border it holds, using common rules set out in the Schengen Borders Code. Travellers from outside the area are checked when they first enter, and from then on can move freely.

    To support this, the EU runs the Schengen Information System, a shared database used by police, border guards and visa officers to exchange alerts about wanted people and stolen documents. It also operates Frontex, the European Border and Coast Guard Agency, which helps member states with border management.

    What it means for travellers

    For visitors from outside Europe, the main practical effect is the Schengen visa. A short-stay visa issued by one member state generally lets you travel across the whole area. For many nationalities no visa is required for short visits, but there is a limit: you can stay for up to 90 days in any 180-day period. That is counted across the entire area, not per country, which surprises many people planning a long trip through several countries.

    The EU is also introducing new systems, such as an electronic entry and exit registration for non-EU travellers and a pre-travel authorisation for visa-exempt visitors. The details and dates have shifted several times, so check official sources before you travel.

    The economic and social effects

    Schengen is part of why Europe's economy works the way it does. Trucks cross borders without long waits, which lowers transport costs. Hundreds of thousands of people live in one country and work in another, the so-called cross-border commuters. Tourism, student exchanges and regional cooperation all benefit.

    Critics point to the strain on external borders and the difficulty of coordinating security. Supporters answer that the answer to those problems is better cooperation, not a return to internal checkpoints.

    Why it matters

    The system depends on trust: each country has to believe that its neighbours manage their part of the border responsibly. When that trust is tested, as it was in the last decade, the arguments tend to be intense. But the daily reality remains remarkable. A student in Vienna can take a train to Munich, a family in Estonia can drive to Poland for the weekend, and nobody needs a stamp in a passport to do it.

    The Story of the Euro: From a Controversial Idea to Everyday Money

    How a single currency went from a political dream to the cash in millions of wallets, and why it has been argued over ever since.

    Every day, hundreds of millions of people pay for coffee, rent and groceries in a currency that did not exist a generation ago. The euro is now so ordinary that it is easy to forget how unlikely it once seemed. For centuries, Europe's money was a patchwork of francs, marks, lire, pesetas and guilders. Creating a single currency took decades of planning, negotiation and political risk.

    Early attempts

    The idea of closer monetary cooperation in Europe is older than the euro by many years. After the collapse of the post-war Bretton Woods system of fixed exchange rates in the early 1970s, European governments looked for ways to limit swings between their currencies. In 1979 they created the European Monetary System, which included an Exchange Rate Mechanism that kept currencies within agreed bands against each other.

    It worked, up to a point. But it also showed the limits of cooperation without a common currency. In 1992 and 1993 speculative pressure forced several currencies, including the British pound and the Italian lira, out of the mechanism. For many policymakers, the lesson was that fixed exchange rates are fragile unless countries commit fully.

    Maastricht and the criteria

    The decisive step came with the Treaty on European Union, signed in Maastricht in 1992 and in force from 1993. It set out a timetable for economic and monetary union and defined entry conditions, now known as the convergence criteria. To join, a country had to show: - Price stability: inflation not much higher than the best performing member states. - Sound public finances: a government deficit below 3 percent of GDP and public debt at or below 60 percent of GDP, or falling towards that level. - Exchange-rate stability: membership of the exchange-rate mechanism without severe tension for at least two years. - Low long-term interest rates: a sign that markets trust the country's economy.

    Each of these was meant to ensure that countries sharing a currency were economically similar enough to make it work. Debates continue about how well those criteria were applied, and about whether they were the right ones.

    Launch

    The euro was introduced on 1 January 1999 as an electronic currency used for accounting and financial markets, with eleven countries taking part. The European Central Bank, in Frankfurt, took charge of monetary policy for the euro area. Banknotes and coins did not appear until 1 January 2002, when people in twelve countries started to handle them. Within weeks, the old national currencies had largely disappeared from circulation.

    The design of the banknotes uses architectural styles from different periods of European history rather than real buildings, deliberately avoiding favouring any one country. Coins have a common side showing the value, and a national side chosen by each issuing country, which is why you can find a coin from Finland or Ireland in your change in Spain.

    The test: the debt crisis

    For its first decade the euro seemed a quiet success. Then, after the global financial crisis of 2008, it faced its hardest test. Several countries, including Greece, Ireland, Portugal and Spain, struggled with high debt, weak banks or both, and borrowing costs rose sharply. Because members no longer had their own currencies, they could not devalue to regain competitiveness, and the monetary union lacked some tools that a country normally has, such as a common fiscal authority.

    In response, Europe built new institutions: a permanent rescue fund known as the European Stability Mechanism, a banking union with common supervision of large banks, and stricter rules for national budgets. In 2012 the ECB's then President, Mario Draghi, said the bank would do "whatever it takes" to preserve the euro, a statement credited with calming markets.

    Who uses it today

    The number of countries using the euro has grown over time as new members have met the criteria and chosen to join. The euro is also used, with agreement, in a few small places outside the EU, and many more countries link their own currency to it. It is the second most widely used currency in international reserves and payments after the US dollar.

    Not every EU country is in the euro. Denmark has a formal opt-out, and others have either chosen to remain outside or are still working towards meeting the criteria.

    What it changed in daily life

    For travellers and businesses, the euro removed exchange fees and exchange-rate risk within the area. Prices became easier to compare between countries. Cross-border trade rose, and the common payments network, SEPA, made bank transfers between countries as simple as domestic ones.

    The continuing argument

    Economists remain divided on how well the euro has served its members. Supporters point to stability, trade and the political bond it creates. Critics say a single interest rate cannot suit every economy at once, and that without shared fiscal tools the system remains vulnerable. Both sides agree on one thing: the euro is more than money. It is a statement about how far European countries are prepared to go together.

    Travelling Europe by Train: A Practical Guide to High-Speed and Night Rail

    There is a particular pleasure in crossing a continent by train. You leave a city centre, watch the landscape change for a few hours, and step out in another country, with no airport transfers and no security queue. Europe has the densest rail network in the world, and with a little planning it can be a very good way to travel.

    A short history

    Passenger rail in Europe is almost two centuries old, but the modern high-speed era began in 1964 in Japan and reached Europe in 1981, when the French TGV started running between Paris and Lyon. Other countries followed with their own systems: Spain's AVE, Germany's ICE, Italy's Frecciarossa and the Eurostar through the Channel Tunnel, which opened in 1994.

    In 1972, Europe also created one of its most beloved travel products. The Interrail pass was launched to celebrate the 50th anniversary of the International Union of Railways, offering young people unlimited travel across a number of countries. It became a rite of passage for generations of backpackers, and a version for non-European residents, called Eurail, followed.

    Advertisement

    High-speed versus regional trains

    High-speed trains typically run at 250 to 320 kilometres per hour on dedicated tracks. They link major city pairs very quickly: Paris to Brussels in about an hour and twenty minutes, Madrid to Barcelona in under three hours, Milan to Rome in around three hours.

    These services nearly always require a seat reservation, and tickets are priced dynamically in many countries. That means booking early can save a large amount of money. Regional trains, by contrast, usually have fixed prices, do not need a reservation, and are the cheapest way to cover shorter distances. They are also where rail passes give the best value.

    Passes or point-to-point tickets?

    Many first-time travellers assume a pass is automatically cheaper. It is not always so. A simple way to decide is to compare: - Count your travel days. Passes are priced per number of travel days within a period. If you only make a few long journeys, point-to-point tickets bought early may be cheaper. - Check reservation fees. High-speed and night trains often charge a seat reservation supplement on top of the pass, which can be significant. - Think about flexibility. A pass lets you change plans, which can be worth the premium if your itinerary is loose. - Look at countries. Some countries have very cheap regional fares, while others, such as Switzerland and the UK, are expensive without a pass.

    Night trains are returning

    For several years many sleeper services were cut, but interest has grown again, partly because of concern about the climate impact of flying. Operators in Austria, Sweden, France and elsewhere have introduced or expanded night routes, including seats, couchettes with six or four bunks, and private sleeper compartments. A night train lets you travel while you sleep and arrive in the centre of a city in the morning, saving a night of accommodation too.

    Climate and comfort

    Per passenger and per kilometre, trains in Europe typically produce far less carbon dioxide than planes or cars, especially on electrified lines. That is one reason that some governments have restricted very short domestic flights where a fast train alternative exists.

    Booking tips

    • Use a multi-country journey planner to check times, but compare prices on the national operator's site, which often has the cheapest fares.
    • Book high-speed trains as soon as the date opens, which can be several months ahead depending on the country.
    • Allow enough time for connections, especially when changing between countries or operators. A tight connection on separate tickets is not protected if you are delayed.
    • Know your rights. In the EU, passengers have rights to information, assistance and compensation for long delays, which are set out in EU regulations and apply on most international journeys.
    • Check strike dates. Rail strikes occur in several countries, and national websites announce them in advance.

    A simple example itinerary

    Imagine starting in London, taking Eurostar to Paris, then a high-speed train to Barcelona in a single day with one change, followed by a night train or fast service along the Mediterranean coast to Italy, and then a train through the Alps to Vienna or Zurich. Each leg is a short step on its own, but together they show the scale of what the network offers.

    Is it worth it?

    Trains are rarely the fastest option for very long distances, and they can cost more than a budget flight. But they bring you into city centres, let you work or look out of the window, and make the journey part of the trip. For many travellers, that is the whole point.

    Every time you land on a European website and are asked about cookies, you are seeing the effect of a single piece of legislation. The General Data Protection Regulation, known as the GDPR, has changed how organisations around the world handle personal data. It is long and technical, but its core ideas are simple.

    Where it came from

    Before 2018, European data protection was governed by a 1995 directive, written when the internet was in its infancy. National laws differed, and the rules did not fit a world of social networks, smartphones and cloud storage. After several years of drafting and debate, the GDPR was adopted in 2016 and became fully applicable on 25 May 2018.

    Unlike a directive, a regulation applies directly in every member state, so the same rules apply from Portugal to Finland. It also applies beyond the EU: if an organisation offers goods or services to people in the EU, or monitors their behaviour, it falls under the law, wherever it is based.

    Advertisement

    What counts as personal data

    The definition is broad. Personal data means any information relating to an identified or identifiable person. A name and email address are obvious examples, but so are location data, an IP address, an online identifier or a photograph. Some categories, such as health data, political opinions, religious beliefs and biometric data, are "special categories" with stricter protection.

    The core principles

    The regulation lists principles that every organisation must follow when handling personal data: - Lawfulness, fairness and transparency: there must be a valid legal reason for processing data, and people must be told about it clearly. - Purpose limitation: data collected for one purpose should not be reused for an unrelated purpose. - Data minimisation: collect only what you need. - Accuracy: keep data correct and up to date. - Storage limitation: do not keep data longer than necessary. - Integrity and confidentiality: protect data with appropriate security. - Accountability: be able to demonstrate compliance.

    The legal bases

    An organisation cannot process personal data simply because it wants to. It needs a lawful basis, and the regulation lists six: consent, a contract, a legal obligation, protecting vital interests, a task in the public interest, and "legitimate interests". Consent has to be freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous, which is why pre-ticked boxes are not acceptable.

    Your rights

    The GDPR gives individuals a set of rights over their data, including: - Access: you can ask what data an organisation holds about you and receive a copy. - Rectification: you can have incorrect data corrected. - Erasure: often called the "right to be forgotten", this lets you ask for deletion in certain situations. - Restriction and objection: you can limit or object to certain processing, such as direct marketing. - Portability: you can receive your data in a usable format and move it to another service. - Rights around automated decisions: you can ask for human involvement where decisions are made solely by automated means with significant effects.

    Organisations generally have one month to respond to such requests.

    The obligations on organisations

    Companies must keep records of their processing, carry out impact assessments for high-risk activities, and build privacy into new products, an idea known as "data protection by design". Public bodies and organisations engaged in large scale monitoring must appoint a data protection officer. If a breach puts people at risk, the organisation must notify the supervisory authority within 72 hours of becoming aware.

    Fines

    The enforcement powers are what made the regulation famous. For the most serious violations, supervisory authorities can impose fines of up to 20 million euros or 4 percent of a company's total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. Several large technology companies have received fines in the hundreds of millions of euros. But authorities also use other tools, such as warnings, orders to change practices, and temporary bans on processing.

    Why the cookie banners?

    The cookie banners you see are mostly connected to a separate law, the ePrivacy Directive, which requires consent before storing or reading information on a user's device, except where it is strictly necessary. The GDPR sets the standard for what valid consent means. Together, they explain why consent screens have become so common, and why regulators have criticised designs that make it hard to refuse.

    Global influence

    The GDPR has become a model. Many countries and regions have adopted privacy laws that borrow its ideas, and global companies often apply its standards worldwide rather than maintaining separate systems. Critics say compliance is costly for small businesses and that enforcement has been uneven. Supporters point out that it gave ordinary people, for the first time, real legal tools to see and control what is done with their information.

    How the Internet Crosses the Ocean: The Hidden World of Undersea Cables

    Satellites get the attention, but almost all international internet traffic travels through glass fibres on the seabed.

    When you send a message to a friend on another continent or stream a film from a server abroad, it is natural to imagine the signal bouncing off a satellite. In reality, the vast majority of international internet traffic, commonly estimated at well over 95 percent, travels through cables lying on the ocean floor. They are the physical backbone of the digital world, and most people never think about them.

    A very old idea

    The first transatlantic telegraph cable was completed in 1858, linking Ireland and Newfoundland. It worked only briefly, but it proved the concept. A durable connection followed in 1866. For about a century, telegraph and then telephone cables carried messages between continents, and the first transatlantic telephone cable, TAT-1, entered service in 1956.

    The big change came in the late 1980s with optical fibre. Instead of copper wires carrying electrical signals, glass fibres carry pulses of light, with far greater capacity and lower loss. The first transatlantic fibre cable began service in 1988, and the network has expanded constantly since then.

    Advertisement

    What a cable looks like

    People often imagine something as thick as a tree trunk. In deep water, a typical cable is about as thick as a garden hose. At its centre are thin glass fibres, surrounded by layers of copper or aluminium for powering equipment, steel wires for strength and plastic insulation. Close to the shore, where the cable is more likely to be damaged by anchors and fishing gear, it is much more heavily armoured and often buried in the seabed.

    Each fibre pair can carry a huge amount of data, and a modern cable contains several pairs. The newest systems have capacities of hundreds of terabits per second.

    How light travels thousands of kilometres

    Light weakens as it travels through glass. To cross an ocean, cables need amplifiers, called repeaters, placed at intervals of several dozen kilometres along the route. These boost the optical signal without converting it back to electricity. The repeaters are powered from the shore through the cable itself, with high voltage delivered over the copper layer.

    Engineers also use techniques that send many separate wavelengths, effectively different colours of light, through the same fibre at once. This is known as wavelength division multiplexing, and it multiplies capacity without laying new cables.

    Laying and repairing

    Special cable-laying ships carry thousands of kilometres of cable in huge tanks. A plough pulled behind the ship buries the cable near the coast, while in deep water it is simply laid on the seabed along a route chosen after careful surveys to avoid steep slopes, earthquake zones and busy fishing areas.

    Damage is surprisingly common. Industry sources estimate that well over a hundred cable faults happen every year around the world, most caused by fishing activity and ship anchors, with some from earthquakes and underwater landslides. Dedicated repair ships stationed around the world recover the damaged section, splice in a new piece and lower it back. Repairs can take days or weeks depending on the weather and the location.

    Who owns them

    Historically, cables were built by consortia of telecommunications companies. In recent years, large technology companies have become major investors, and some now own or lease their own private cables to connect their data centres. This change has altered both the routes and the speed at which new systems are deployed.

    Europe's role

    Europe is a major hub. Many transatlantic cables land on the coasts of Ireland, the United Kingdom, France, Spain and Portugal, while others connect the Mediterranean to Asia through the Red Sea, and Nordic routes link Europe with northern Asia. Marseille, for example, has become one of the most important landing points because it connects Europe to Africa, the Middle East and Asia.

    Resilience and security

    Because so much depends on a small number of physical routes, protecting cables has become a policy issue. Governments worry about both accidental damage and deliberate sabotage, and several incidents in the Baltic and Red Seas in recent years have drawn attention to the problem. The EU has encouraged measures such as route diversity, faster repair capacity and better monitoring of vulnerable areas.

    What it means for you

    Next time a video call connects instantly with someone across an ocean, remember what is happening. A pulse of light is running through a glass thread on the seabed, passing through amplifiers powered from land, probably crossing under the path of ships and whales on its way. It is a large piece of engineering that works so well that it is invisible.

    Inside CERN: What the Large Hadron Collider Is Looking For

    A 27-kilometre ring under the Swiss-French border smashes protons together. We explain how it works and what it has found so far.

    Beneath the fields and villages on the border between France and Switzerland, near Geneva, lies the largest machine ever built. The Large Hadron Collider, or LHC, is a ring of superconducting magnets about 27 kilometres around, buried roughly a hundred metres below the surface. Its purpose is to answer a simple-sounding question: what is the universe made of, and how does it work at the smallest scales?

    The organisation behind it

    CERN, the European Organization for Nuclear Research, was founded in 1954 by twelve European countries as a way to rebuild scientific cooperation after the Second World War. Today it has over twenty member states, with thousands of visiting scientists from all over the world. Its name comes from the French "Conseil Européen pour la Recherche Nucléaire", though its work today is in particle physics rather than nuclear power.

    CERN is also where the World Wide Web began. In 1989, the British scientist Tim Berners-Lee, working there, proposed a system for sharing information between researchers, and in 1993 CERN made the web technology available to everyone, free of charge.

    Advertisement

    How the collider works

    The LHC accelerates beams of protons, which are particles found in the nucleus of every atom, to almost the speed of light. Two beams travel in opposite directions in separate pipes inside the ring, steered by more than a thousand powerful superconducting magnets. These magnets are cooled to about minus 271 degrees Celsius, colder than outer space, using liquid helium.

    At four points around the ring, the beams are brought together so that protons collide. Each collision releases a burst of energy, and that energy can turn into new particles, according to Einstein's famous relation between mass and energy. Giant detectors surrounding the collision points record what comes out. The two largest, ATLAS and CMS, are as tall as multi-storey buildings.

    The Standard Model

    Physicists describe the known particles and forces, except gravity, with a framework called the Standard Model. It lists the fundamental building blocks of matter, such as quarks and electrons, and the particles that carry forces. For decades, it had been remarkably successful, with one missing piece: it could not explain why some particles have mass.

    The Higgs boson

    In the 1960s, several theorists proposed that an invisible field fills the whole universe, and that particles gain mass by interacting with it. The field would come with its own particle, now called the Higgs boson. Finding it became one of the main goals of the LHC.

    On 4 July 2012, CERN announced that the ATLAS and CMS experiments had both observed a new particle consistent with the Higgs boson. The following year, Peter Higgs and François Englert were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics for their theoretical work. Since then, measurements of its properties have continued, and so far they match the predictions of the Standard Model.

    What we still do not know

    The Standard Model is incomplete. Observations in astronomy show that ordinary matter makes up only around five percent of the universe. The rest is thought to be dark matter and dark energy, neither of which the model can explain. It also does not tell us why there is more matter than antimatter, or why neutrinos have mass.

    The LHC searches for hints of new physics, such as particles that could make up dark matter or signs of extra dimensions. So far it has not found clear evidence of them, which is itself an important result because it rules out many proposed theories.

    Upgrades and the future

    The collider has run in periods called "runs", separated by long shutdowns for maintenance and upgrades. A major upgrade project, the High-Luminosity LHC, is intended to increase the number of collisions substantially, allowing physicists to study rare processes in more detail. CERN is also studying the possibility of a much larger future collider, which would be a very expensive and long-term project that is still being debated.

    More than physics

    The technologies developed for particle physics have had wide effects. Medical scanners, cancer treatments using beams of particles, advanced computing grids for sharing data and new materials have all benefited. Training thousands of young scientists and engineers is another lasting contribution.

    Visiting

    CERN welcomes visitors. The Globe of Science and Innovation near the main site hosts exhibitions, and guided tours are available when booked in advance. Standing in a control room where physicists monitor beams moving at nearly the speed of light is a good reminder that curiosity can be an enormous collective project.

    Why the Alps Are Losing Their Glaciers, and Why It Matters Downstream

    The Alps are one of the most recognisable landscapes on Earth. For centuries, travellers, poets and painters have been drawn by their white summits and slow rivers of ice. Today those glaciers are shrinking fast, and the change affects more than the view.

    What a glacier is

    A glacier is a large body of ice that forms on land where more snow falls in winter than melts in summer. Over years, the snow is compressed into ice, and once the mass is thick enough it starts to flow slowly downhill under its own weight. The upper part, where snow accumulates, is called the accumulation zone, and the lower part, where ice melts, is the ablation zone. A glacier is healthy when the two are in balance. If the melting grows larger than the accumulation, it retreats and thins.

    What the measurements say

    Glaciers in the Alps have been monitored for well over a century, and several of them have records of annual changes in length, area and mass. The overall trend since the middle of the nineteenth century, the end of what is called the Little Ice Age, has been a retreat. But the pace has increased sharply in recent decades.

    Advertisement

    In Switzerland, the national monitoring network has reported dramatic losses. In 2022, glaciers lost a record amount of ice, after a winter with little snow and a summer with heat waves. The following year, they lost much more, and over the two years combined, they lost around ten percent of their remaining volume, according to the Swiss measurements. Scientists say that this is an extraordinarily rapid decline. Many smaller glaciers have already disappeared.

    Why it is happening

    The main driver is a rise in temperature. The Alps are warming faster than the global average, in part because of local feedbacks. Snow and ice reflect most of the sunlight that falls on them, and when they melt, the darker rock or soil underneath absorbs more heat, which speeds up melting. Another factor is Saharan dust and soot carried by wind, which darken the surface of the snow and ice and make it absorb more heat.

    Changing precipitation also matters. Less winter snow means less accumulation, and rain falling instead of snow at higher altitudes can accelerate melting.

    The water tower of Europe

    Mountains are often called the water towers of the continent. Rivers such as the Rhine, the Rhône, the Danube and the Po all begin in the Alps, and together they supply drinking water, irrigation, hydropower and navigation to tens of millions of people.

    Glaciers act as a buffer. In hot, dry summers, when rainfall is low, meltwater keeps rivers flowing. As glaciers shrink, there is a temporary increase in meltwater, but over the longer term the amount of water they release will drop. Researchers refer to this as "peak water", the point after which the contribution begins to decline. For many Alpine glaciers, scientists believe it has either passed or is close.

    Effects on people and infrastructure

    The consequences are visible in several areas: - Hydropower: Swiss and Austrian power plants depend on summer meltwater. Changes in timing and volume require adaptation. - Tourism: Ski resorts at lower altitudes already struggle with less natural snow, and glacier skiing is increasingly difficult. - Natural hazards: As permafrost thaws, rock faces become less stable, increasing the risk of rockfalls and landslides. New lakes form at the edge of retreating glaciers and can burst suddenly. - Agriculture: Farms in lowland valleys may face irrigation shortages in dry summers.

    What can be done

    Experts distinguish between two broad responses. The first is reducing greenhouse gas emissions, which determines how much warming occurs in the long run. Studies suggest that even if warming is limited, a large part of today's glacier ice will be lost, but the amount that remains depends strongly on how much temperature rises.

    The second is adaptation. Some places have tried covering small areas of ice with reflective blankets to slow melting, and this can work on a very small scale for ski slopes, but it is not a solution for whole glaciers. More realistic adaptation includes building water storage, improving monitoring of hazards and planning energy systems to cope with changing river flows.

    A landscape in motion

    If you visit the Alps, you may see marker signs along the paths showing where a glacier's edge stood decades ago, far above the current end of the ice. Those signs are an unusually clear form of data. They show, in a few minutes of walking, what takes scientists years of measurement to confirm: a mountain landscape that people thought of as permanent is changing within a single lifetime.

    How the European Central Bank Sets Interest Rates, and Why It Affects Your Wallet

    Why one institution in Frankfurt can change the price of your mortgage, your savings account and your groceries.

    When news reports say "the ECB has raised rates" or "the ECB has held rates steady", it can sound distant and technical. But the decision made in Frankfurt affects a large number of everyday things, from what a family pays on its mortgage to what a business pays on a loan, and how fast prices rise in the shops.

    What the ECB is and what it does

    The European Central Bank is the central bank for the euro area. Together with the national central banks of the member states that use the euro, it forms what is called the Eurosystem. Its main job, set out in the EU treaties, is to maintain price stability. In practice, the ECB has defined this as keeping inflation at 2 percent over the medium term.

    The ECB is independent. The treaties forbid it from taking instructions from governments or EU institutions, and they forbid it from financing governments directly. The reasoning is that if politicians could order a central bank to lower rates before an election, inflation would be harder to control.

    Advertisement

    The rate that matters

    The ECB sets three key interest rates. The most important in recent years has been the deposit facility rate, which is what banks earn when they park spare money at the central bank overnight. The others are the main refinancing rate, used when banks borrow from the ECB for a week, and the marginal lending facility rate, a higher rate for emergency overnight borrowing.

    These rates form the base for the cost of money in the euro area. When the ECB raises them, borrowing gets more expensive throughout the economy, and when it lowers them, borrowing gets cheaper.

    How a decision reaches your pocket

    The effect passes through several channels: - Mortgages and loans: Many loans are linked to market rates that follow the ECB's decisions. In countries where variable-rate mortgages are common, the effect is quick. Where fixed-rate mortgages dominate, it arrives more slowly as people renew. - Savings: Banks tend to offer higher interest on deposits when central bank rates are higher, although they often adjust savings rates more slowly than loan rates. - Business investment: Higher borrowing costs make companies think twice about expanding, hiring and investing. - The exchange rate: Higher rates can make a currency more attractive to investors, which tends to strengthen it and lower the price of imports.

    Why raise rates at all?

    Imagine an economy where demand for goods and services is growing faster than the economy can supply them. Prices rise. By raising interest rates, the central bank makes borrowing and spending less attractive and saving more attractive. Demand cools, and inflation eases.

    The risk is that if it goes too far, the economy slows too much, businesses cut jobs, and unemployment rises. Setting rates is a balancing act under uncertainty, because the effects take many months to appear.

    Beyond interest rates

    When rates are already very low and the economy still needs support, central banks can use other tools. After the financial crisis of 2008 and again during the pandemic, the ECB bought large amounts of government and corporate bonds, a policy known as quantitative easing. This pushes down longer-term interest rates and adds money to the financial system. When the policy is reversed, it is called quantitative tightening.

    The ECB also supervises the largest banks in the euro area, as part of the banking union created after the debt crisis.

    How decisions are made

    The main decision-making body is the Governing Council. It consists of the six members of the Executive Board and the governors of the national central banks of the euro area countries. It meets regularly, usually every six weeks, to set monetary policy. After each meeting on rates, the President holds a press conference to explain the reasoning. Analysts and journalists watch these words closely, because the signals about future policy can move markets as much as the decision itself.

    Why different countries feel it differently

    One interest rate must serve many economies. A rate that is right for a fast-growing country with high inflation may be too high for a slow one with low inflation. This is one of the oldest criticisms of the single currency. Supporters say that economic differences between regions exist within every large country, and that the benefits of a single currency outweigh the costs.

    What to take from this

    You do not need to follow every decision to benefit from understanding the mechanism. If rates rise, expect borrowing to cost more and savings to pay a bit more. If they fall, the opposite. And remember that the central bank is trying to guard something that cannot be seen directly: the steady purchasing power of the money in your pocket.

    The Netherlands is a small country. It is slightly larger than Switzerland in area, with roughly eighteen million people, and a quarter of its land lies below sea level. Yet it is among the largest exporters in the world, home to Europe's biggest seaport and to some of the continent's most important trading companies. The reasons are partly geographical and partly historical.

    Location and water

    The country sits at the mouth of three of Europe's major rivers, the Rhine, the Meuse and the Scheldt. These rivers lead deep into the industrial heartland of Germany, Belgium and France. For centuries, goods have moved along them, and the Dutch ports lie at the point where river transport meets the sea.

    Living with water has also made the Dutch skilled at engineering. They have built dikes, drained lakes to create farmland, and constructed enormous storm surge barriers. The Delta Works, built after a catastrophic flood in 1953, are among the most ambitious flood protection systems in the world. This tradition of managing water produced expertise in dredging, shipping and hydraulic engineering that is still exported today.

    The Golden Age

    In the seventeenth century, the Dutch Republic became one of the richest countries in Europe. Its merchants dominated trade in spices, textiles and other goods, and its ships connected Europe with Asia, Africa and the Americas. This period, usually called the Dutch Golden Age, was also marked by art, science and philosophy, from Rembrandt and Vermeer to the physicist Christiaan Huygens.

    Behind this prosperity lay financial innovation. In 1602, the Dutch East India Company, known as the VOC, was founded. It is often described as the first company in the world to issue shares to the general public, and its shares were traded on the Amsterdam exchange, which is considered one of the earliest stock exchanges. By allowing many investors to share risk and capital, the model made it possible to finance long and dangerous voyages.

    The Golden Age also had a dark side. The Dutch were heavily involved in colonial exploitation and the transatlantic slave trade, and the country continues to examine this history in its museums and public debate.

    The modern port

    Today the Port of Rotterdam is the largest port in Europe, handling hundreds of millions of tonnes of cargo each year. It stretches for dozens of kilometres along the Maas river toward the North Sea, and includes container terminals, oil and chemical complexes, and facilities for bulk goods. Ships of the largest class can enter it, and from there goods are moved onwards by barge, rail and road to markets across Europe.

    Rotterdam is also more than a transit point. The port is the centre of a large industrial cluster, including refining and chemicals, and the Netherlands is now trying to turn it into a hub for hydrogen, offshore wind power and carbon capture as part of the energy transition.

    Schiphol and logistics

    Another important gateway is Amsterdam Schiphol Airport, one of the busiest in Europe. Along with the ports, motorway and rail networks, it forms an integrated logistics system. The Dutch have used their position to create a reputation as the "gateway to Europe", where many companies locate their distribution centres.

    Agriculture and technology

    A surprising fact is that the Netherlands is one of the world's largest exporters of agricultural products, second only to the United States by value. This is possible through intensive, high-tech farming: greenhouses with climate control, precision irrigation and advanced breeding. Flowers, vegetables and dairy products are important exports. At the same time, the country has a thriving technology sector, including ASML, the company that makes the most advanced machines used to manufacture computer chips.

    Open economy, open questions

    Trade is central to the Dutch economy, and the country is strongly in favour of open markets and the EU single market. That openness also creates exposure to global shocks. A slowdown in world trade, supply chain disruptions or changing tariffs quickly affect Dutch ports and exporters.

    The country faces other challenges too. Housing is costly and scarce in the big cities, and environmental limits on nitrogen emissions have created tension in the farming sector. Because the Dutch economy is so intertwined with the rest of the world, such debates often have European and even global consequences.

    The lesson

    The Dutch story shows that geography is not destiny but is a starting point. A country with few natural resources but a good location, strong institutions, financial creativity and a willingness to trade openly can punch far above its weight. It also shows that long-term success requires constant adaptation, from the sailing ships of the seventeenth century to the container ships and chip machines of the twenty-first.

    A Short History of the Eurovision Song Contest

    How a modest experiment in live television in 1956 became one of the most watched non-sporting events in the world.

    By Editorial Team20 October 20263 min read

    Every spring, tens of millions of people across Europe and beyond sit down to watch an evening of songs, costumes and elaborate stagecraft. The Eurovision Song Contest is both loved and mocked, and it is one of the longest-running television programmes in the world. Its history says a lot about Europe itself.

    An experiment in broadcasting

    The contest was created by the European Broadcasting Union, an alliance of public service broadcasters formed in 1950. In the 1950s, the EBU was looking for a way to test the technology of live international television and to bring countries together after the war. Inspired by Italy's Sanremo Music Festival, it proposed a song competition where each participating country would send an original song performed live.

    The first contest took place on 24 May 1956 in Lugano, Switzerland, with seven countries taking part. Each country sent two songs, which is a rule that was later dropped. The winner was Switzerland, with the song "Refrain" performed by Lys Assia. It was a small event that was largely broadcast on radio and a limited number of television sets, but the format worked.

    Advertisement

    The rules, in brief

    Although details have changed over the years, the basic principles are stable: - Each country is represented by a broadcaster that is a member of the EBU, which selects the act and song. - Songs must be original and have a limited length, and live vocals are required. - Voting combines two groups: a panel of professional jurors in each country and a public vote, with points awarded from 1 to 12. - The winning country hosts the next contest, which is why the event moves from city to city.

    Participants do not have to be in the EU, or even geographically in Europe. Countries such as Israel and Australia have taken part, because the criterion is membership of the EBU, not location.

    The breakthrough moments

    Many of the most famous moments belong to the contest's history. In 1974, the Swedish group ABBA won in Brighton with "Waterloo", which launched them toward global stardom. In 1988, a young Canadian singer, Céline Dion, won for Switzerland. In 1967, the British singer Sandie Shaw won the contest barefoot, and in 1969 an unusual four-way tie occurred between Spain, the United Kingdom, the Netherlands and France.

    The contest has also been a platform for artists to make a mark with theatrical and technological innovation, from the first electronic effects to the enormous LED walls of modern productions.

    Politics and language

    Although the organisers insist that the contest is non-political, it has never been completely separate from politics. Countries have withdrawn, returned or been excluded for various reasons over the decades. Voting patterns have been analysed by scholars, who have found that neighbouring countries and those with cultural ties often give each other high scores.

    Language rules have also changed. For several years, songs had to be performed in an official national language. Since 1999, with a short interruption, countries have been free to choose any language, and the proportion of songs in English has grown. Some countries now actively choose their own language as a cultural statement, while others see English as a way of reaching a wider audience.

    A community of fans

    Eurovision has an unusually devoted fan culture. Fans follow national selections for months, host viewing parties and debate the odds. The contest has also developed a strong following among LGBTQ+ audiences, who celebrate its spirit of flamboyance and its message of inclusion. Several winners over the years have been celebrated as symbols of tolerance.

    Winning formulas?

    People often ask whether there is a formula. Analysts say that songs that do well typically have a strong staging idea, are memorable within the first few seconds, and benefit from a good position in the running order. But the history of the contest is full of surprises, with ballads, rock, folk, opera-inspired songs and electronic music all winning.

    Why it endures

    In an era of streaming and fragmented audiences, it is rare for a programme to bring so many people to watch the same thing at the same time. Eurovision offers a shared experience, a moment when the continent argues cheerfully about taste. For all its glitter, it shows that culture and cooperation can coexist with rivalry, as long as the rules are clear and the music is loud enough.

    The European Coffeehouse: How Cafés Shaped Ideas, Business and City Life

    From Venice and Vienna to London and Paris, the café has been a workplace, a newsroom and a debating chamber for more than three centuries.

    In most European cities, there is a place where someone has been sitting with a cup of coffee and a newspaper for hours, and nobody minds. The café is such an everyday institution that it is easy to forget how important it has been in the history of ideas, business and city life.

    How coffee arrived

    Coffee came to Europe from the Ottoman world during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, through the trading ports of the Mediterranean, particularly Venice. At first it was regarded with suspicion by some, including some church authorities, but its popularity grew and the first coffee houses opened in Venice in the middle of the seventeenth century. A well known story says that a pope tasted it and approved, though historians treat the anecdote with caution.

    By the 1650s, coffeehouses had appeared in England, and in the following decades they spread across the continent, reaching Paris, Vienna, Amsterdam and many other cities.

    Advertisement

    The "penny universities"

    In seventeenth-century London, coffeehouses charged a small entry fee, often a penny, for which you got a cup of coffee and access to conversation, newspapers and news from abroad. They became known as "penny universities" because you could learn a lot by listening. Unlike taverns, they encouraged sober discussion, and different houses attracted different groups: scientists, merchants, writers, politicians.

    Business was done there. Edward Lloyd's coffeehouse in London was a place where shipowners and merchants gathered to arrange insurance for voyages. It grew into Lloyd's of London, still one of the world's best-known insurance markets. Similarly, the origins of the London Stock Exchange are linked to brokers who gathered in coffeehouses around Exchange Alley.

    Places of public debate

    Philosophers have long argued that coffeehouses helped form a new kind of public sphere, where people of different ranks could discuss politics, science and literature on relatively equal terms. In France, cafés such as the Procope in Paris were associated with Enlightenment figures like Voltaire, Diderot and Rousseau, and later with revolutionary debates. The authorities were aware of this: in 1675, King Charles II of England tried to suppress coffeehouses as centres of sedition, though the proclamation was soon withdrawn after public protest.

    Vienna and the coffeehouse as a way of life

    Few cities embrace the café as much as Vienna. According to legend, coffee was brought to the city after the Ottoman siege of 1683, though historians point out that coffee had arrived earlier through trade. What is clear is that by the nineteenth century, Viennese coffeehouses were central to cultural life. Writers, musicians, artists and political thinkers made them their offices and salons. Painters met there, journalists wrote there, and chess players could spend a whole day over a single cup.

    In 2011, UNESCO added Viennese coffee house culture to its list of intangible cultural heritage, describing it as a place where time and space are consumed but only coffee appears on the bill. That phrase captures the idea that you are paying not just for a drink, but for a place to sit, read and think.

    Different traditions across the continent

    Each country developed its own café style: - Italy: The espresso bar, where coffee is quickly drunk standing at the counter, shaped everyday routines. The espresso machine was developed in Italy in the early twentieth century, and it influenced coffee culture around the world. - France: The pavement café, with chairs facing the street, encouraged people-watching, and became associated with writers and artists in Paris. - Sweden and the Nordic countries: The tradition of "fika" is a daily coffee break with something sweet, shared with colleagues and friends. - Central Europe: Grand historic cafés in Budapest, Prague and Trieste preserve nineteenth-century interiors of marble tables and mirrors.

    The modern café

    Today, cafés have a new role as informal offices. Freelancers, students and remote workers open laptops and stay for hours, using Wi-Fi as much as coffee. Some cafés welcome this and others discourage it, but the underlying pattern is familiar. People have always used cafés as a "third place", somewhere that is neither home nor workplace, where they can be around others without any obligation.

    A cup of history

    The next time you sit down in a European café, you are taking part in a very old tradition. The tables, the newspapers, the quiet argument in the corner all connect to people who sat in similar places centuries ago, trading news, selling insurance, writing manifestos and simply enjoying the pleasure of not being in a hurry.
